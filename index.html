AI for kids · 2026

Little AIKi

Gen AI native playground fostering kids' agency and creativity · DoranDoran

Not just a game: a curriculum built on the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and European Union (EU) AI Literacy Framework and AI4K12, turned into play that is safe, easy and fun.

ResultWeb prototype piloted with about 30 families; selected to pitch to X, The Moonshot Factory (Google X) in Sep 2026.

Read the case study
Role: CEO & co-founder · led curriculum and product design end to endTeam: 3 co-founders + freelance game developerAges 5–10 · Korean, English, GermanFormerly Wisdom Island

Problem

Children meet AI before they can question it. Popular apps for this age teach letters and numbers, but none teach a child to ask why a machine answered the way it did.

Built on research

Every zone and adventure maps to an established AI literacy curriculum, so the game is the delivery, not the content:

  • OECD/European Commission AI Literacy Framework (2026) ↗Competences for engaging with, creating with and managing AI
  • AI4K12 grade-band progressions ↗Big Ideas 1–5 at the kindergarten to grade 2 (K–2) level
  • PopBots, Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AAAI) 2019 ↗Teaching young children AI through hands-on play
  • Druga et al., Interaction Design and Children (IDC) 2017 ↗How children perceive and talk to AI agents

AI Literacy (AILit) content: OECD/European Commission (2026), Empowering Learners for the Age of AI, Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0). This is an adaptation by DoranDoran; the adaptation is not endorsed by the OECD or the European Commission.

Process

  • Designed the Others–Self–AI (OSA) framework: notice bias in Others, then in Self, then in AI.
  • Mapped five ethics pillars across three age bands, starting with a first stage built for 5–6-year-olds: one gesture per action, short spoken lines, no reading required.
  • No penalties and no labels: children reflect through Socratic dialogue with a companion character.
  • Turned EU AI Act and Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) requirements into design rules, from data collection to how the AI companion is allowed to speak.

Key decisions

No scores, penalties or labels.

A five-year-old should never be told she is "biased". The companion reflects her choices back as questions instead.

Parent reports describe, they don't diagnose.

"Often chose the bigger friend" instead of a bias label, so parents don't read a report as a verdict on their child.

Collect as little as possible.

The pilot asked parents only for country and child age, and families never see anyone else's results.

AI never blocks play.

Visual feedback is instant; the companion's AI-generated line is skipped if it takes longer than half a second.

Result

A web prototype pilot of the earlier version (Wisdom Island): about 30 families, with parents reporting only country and child age. Selected through TUM Venture Labs to pitch to X, The Moonshot Factory (Google X), as it explores moonshots in education (Sep 2026).

Why it matters

Within five years, most children will talk to AI before they can read. They will need to question it, not just use it. Little AIKi teaches that habit from age five.

Next

Replace the prototype's fixed rules with the adaptive dialogue engine, make compliance rules configurable per country, and let children explain their reasoning out loud instead of picking an option.

AI literacy · 2025

AI4KIDs

Product design · TUM

ResultPrototype presented and graded 1.0, the top mark in the German system.

Read the case study
Role: product design · process design · AI literacy testAges 10–14 · TUM · 01/2025

Problem

When AI literacy was only starting to enter German education, there was little structured, age-appropriate material for 10–14-year-olds.

Process

  • Built each lesson on a feed up → feed forward → feedback loop, so learners always know their goal, next step and progress.
  • Moved from concepts to practice: what AI is, how neural networks work, a quiz, a block-coding task and a Teachable Machine task.
  • Designed an AI literacy test to see where each learner stands.

Key decisions

A feedback loop in every lesson.

Online learners have no teacher beside them, so each lesson states the goal, the next step and how they're doing.

Build, don't just watch.

Every concept is followed by a hands-on task with a real tool: block coding, then training a model in Teachable Machine.

Test first, then place.

An AI literacy test shows where each learner starts, so the course can meet them there.

Result

Presented the prototype and received a 1.0, the highest grade in the German system.

AI for good · 2026

InitiativGruppe Assistant

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) chatbot for a Munich NGO supporting migrant women and children · TUM Social AI

ResultDemoed at TUM Social AI Demo Day (Apr 2026) and handed over to InitiativGruppe.

Read the case study
Role: problem definition · product flow · process design · frontendTeam: TUM Social AI project team10/2025 – 04/2026 · delivered to partner

Problem

InitiativGruppe supports migrant women and children in Munich with German classes, registration and counselling. Its small team answers the same questions again and again, while the people asking often can't find the answers on the website, in a language they don't read well yet. The assistant takes those routine questions off the team.

Process

  • Defined requirements with the NGO: German- and mobile-first, multilingual answers, WordPress-ready, low running cost.
  • Designed the flow from question to answer with hybrid search (keywords + RAG) over the NGO's public content, refreshed by regular crawling.
  • Built the frontend.

Key decisions

Hybrid search, not a free-form chatbot.

Keywords plus RAG over the NGO's own pages, so answers about courses, registration and fees come from what the NGO actually published.

Fit the NGO, not the other way round.

A small nonprofit can't run new infrastructure, so the assistant reuses its WordPress site and free tiers.

Keep answers current.

Regular crawling updates the knowledge base when the NGO changes its pages, without anyone maintaining it by hand.

Result

Demonstrated live at TUM Social AI Demo Day (April 2026) and handed over to InitiativGruppe.

Next

Rollout is the partner's decision. The next step is the WordPress integration and tracking which questions the assistant can't answer yet.

Safe AI · 2026

Smart Kids, Safe AI

A research-based playbook for parents · TUM

ResultAn illustrated playbook with five scenarios and a 60-second explainer video.

Read the case study
Role: author · research, scenario writing, AI-assisted illustration & layoutParents of 4–7-year-olds · Munich · 09/2026

Problem

A four-year-old talks to a voice assistant before she can read and taps into autoplay before she can choose for herself. Blocking AI or simply allowing it no longer works, and parents are left without a clear role.

Process

  • Built on developmental psychology (Piaget's preoperational stage) and guardrail-centred design rather than prohibition.
  • Grounded legal examples in the EU AI Act, Digital Services Act (DSA) and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), with German data on young children's media use.
  • Wrote five scenarios with one Munich child, Ria, across YouTube and a smart speaker: scenario, struggle, advice, takeaway.
  • Chose a guide over a paper because parents need something usable at home.

Key decisions

A guide, not a paper.

Parents need something they can use the same evening, so every scenario ends with one small action.

One age band, 4–7.

I first covered a wider range, then narrowed it so every piece of advice fits one developmental stage.

Guardrails, not bans.

Blocking AI teaches nothing. The playbook helps a child stay the one in charge instead.

Result

An illustrated playbook where every scenario ends with one small action a parent can try that day, plus a 60-second explainer video.

Read the case study
Solo project: research design, analysis, presentationOpen University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD) · 32,593 learnersLearning Analytics · TUM · Feb 2026

Problem

The old formula was simple: attend, submit, get good grades. With learning moved online and generative AI in every student's hands, doing what is asked may no longer be what sets learners apart. If agency matters more, teachers need to see it, and course design needs to reward it.

Research questions

  1. RQ1 · Variable validityAre activity volume and exploration breadth distinct dimensions in learning management system (LMS) log data?
  2. RQ2 · CorrelationDoes compliance (meeting deadlines) predict passing, and does agency (broad exploration) relate more strongly to distinction?
  3. RQ3 · TypologyDo learner groups built from both dimensions differ in dropout and final achievement?

Method

DimensionMetricWhat it asks
CompliancepunctualityTotal clicksDid they do the required activities?
Inverse of delay daysDid they meet deadlines?
AgencyproactivityPre-course clicksDid they start without being told?
Unique resourcesDid they explore beyond the requirements?
  1. Min-max scaling
  2. K-means clustering, k = 4
  3. ANOVA and chi-square validation

Findings

  • High achievers · 8%Highest compliance and agency. Most pass, many reach distinction.
  • Diligent followers · 24%On time and on path, rarely off it. Most pass, few reach distinction.
  • Passive learners · 50%Do the minimum when reminded. Hover between fail and pass.
  • At-risk · 18%Activity stops within two to three weeks. Mostly withdrawn.

Compared with the diligent group, high achievers were 1.3× as compliant but 1.8× as agentic. Agency, not compliance, separates the top tier. Cluster differences in grades were significant (ANOVA, F = 3079.49, p < .001).

What it means

For educators

Flag learners with no activity in the first two to three weeks. Release materials early and offer optional advanced resources. Assess what learners discover, not only what they submit.

For learners

Compliance is the foundation; agency is the differentiator. Submit on time, then go further: read ahead, explore what isn't required, and use AI to widen the scope.

Limits

Clicks measure volume, not depth, and log data can't tell disengagement from slow, careful thinking. Next steps would add richer signals such as speech, eye-tracking or typing patterns.

Designing Interactive AI for human agency, beyond explainability

From black box to control panel

Only about 5% of studies let users actually change the model. Most keep people as data or feedback providers.

Key points
  • Critical analysis of Raees et al. (2024), a review of 54 studies on interactive AI.
  • Updated it with a structured search: 15 papers from 2020–2025 in four themes (user agency, trust and overreliance, embodied and extended reality (XR) AI, high-stakes domains).
  • Main gap: only about 5% of studies let users actually change the model. Most keep people as data or feedback providers.
  • Critique: prompting a large language model (LLM) feels like control but never touches the model. With agentic AI, the question flips to how to keep human agency at all.

2025 · Paper analysis · Chair of Responsible Data Science, TUM · team of four

LLMs for explainability: understanding and transparency for everyone?

Explaining the explainer

LLMs can turn explainable AI (XAI) outputs into plain language for everyone, but no one can yet measure whether the translation is faithful.

Key points
  • Analysis of Bilal et al. (2025), a survey of how LLMs can make AI decisions explainable.
  • The problem: in user studies, over 30% of people could not understand XAI outputs such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) well enough to use them, even for simple tasks.
  • The opportunity: an LLM can translate those outputs into plain language, tuned to the audience, with follow-up questions.
  • The limits: hallucinated or biased explanations, weaker results outside English, no agreed way to measure explanation quality, and no replacement for domain experts.
